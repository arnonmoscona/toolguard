# Surprise-factor protocol

Arnon's design, 2026-08-08. Before a change is implemented, a blinded agent estimates which files the change will touch and where it will concentrate. After implementation, actual is compared to estimate. **The ratio of actual to expected is the alarm; the diagnosis of each surprise is the finding.** This is a refinement of the co-change measure — instead of asking which files change together, it asks whether the change surface matched what a competent person would have predicted from the requirement.

The instrument is applied across the seven in-scope TOO-45 punch-list items, which gives it a series of mostly small changes to be evaluated over. Arnon's separate proposal — modified co-change `n/(n-1)` — is recorded per item on the same data and evaluated at the end. Neither measure is trusted before that.

## What the estimator sees, and what it must not see

Inline in its prompt, with no repository access:

- the ticket text
- the file inventory: path, line count, and each Python module's **first docstring line only**
- the declared layer map from `.pyscn.toml`

That is the "lazy human" baseline — a person who read the ticket and skimmed the tree. It sees no code, no imports, no call graph, no git history, no other TOO-45 material.

Generated by `scratchpad/build_estimator_briefing.py`, which reads only the four scanned directories and `.pyscn.toml` and writes only to the scratchpad. Regenerated per item so the inventory reflects the tree as it stands at that moment.

## What the estimator returns

Pre-registered structure, fixed here:

1. **Predicted touch set** — every file it expects to be modified, added or deleted, each with a one-line reason and a confidence of `high` / `medium` / `low`.
2. **Concentration set** — the subset it expects the change to be centred in. This is the "where does the work actually live" judgement a team gives a product manager.
3. **Expected counts** — files modified, added, deleted, split into production and test.
4. **Named uncertainties** — what it would need to see to be more confident. This is scored separately and is the most useful field for diagnosing instrument error versus architecture error.

## Scoring, fixed before the first result

Let `P` be the predicted set and `A` the actual set.

| term | definition |
|---|---|
| **hit** | `P ∩ A` |
| **surprise** | `A − P` — touched but not predicted |
| **overshoot** | `P − A` — predicted but not touched |
| ~~surprise ratio~~ | ~~`|A| / |P|`~~ — **DROPPED, 2026-08-09, on Arnon's acceptance.** Four items in, ranked by ratio the order was 05, 15, 04, 01; ranked by recall it was 04, 15, 01, 05 — the extremes inverted every time, because the ratio rewards naming few files rather than naming the right ones. It never once agreed with the measure of whether the change surface was actually foreseen. |
| **recall** | `hits / |A|` — **the headline.** What fraction of the real change was foreseen. |
| **precision** | `hits / |P|` — carried so a predictor cannot win by naming everything. |

**Production and test files are scored separately and never pooled.** Test co-change is near-deterministic and would inflate both sides; a change that surprises only in its tests is not the same finding as one that surprises in the engine.

### Every surprise gets a cause, and only two causes are alarms

This is the part that makes the number mean anything. A bad estimator and bad architecture produce the same ratio; without this split the measure cannot tell them apart.

| cause | alarm? | meaning |
|---|---|---|
| **E — estimator ignorance** | no | nothing in the briefing could have revealed it. Instrument noise. |
| **C — hidden coupling** | **YES** | the code has a dependency a competent reader would not have predicted. This is the finding the measure exists to catch. |
| **P — prose coupling** | **YES** | the file changed only because a comment or docstring named the thing being changed. Added after item 05, where it was 31% of the whole touch set. Its own category because the remedy is shorter comments, not better structure. Classified by AST comparison with docstrings stripped, never by eye. |
| **S — scope creep** | no, but on me | I did more than the ticket asked. A discipline signal, not an architecture signal. |
| **D — latent defect** | **YES** | the change uncovered a pre-existing defect that had to be fixed. |

A high ratio driven by `E` means the briefing is too thin. A high ratio driven by `C` or `D` is the alarm Arnon is after.

### The conflict of interest, and the guard

The estimator is blind; **I am not.** I am the implementer and I assign the causes, and `C` and `D` flatter me while `S` does not. Guard: for at least two of the seven items, a **separate blind adjudicator** classifies the surprises from the diff and the briefing alone, with no knowledge of which category means what. Disagreement between us is itself reported.

### The leak that must be reported, not hidden

**Several of these tickets name their own target files.** #05 lists six modules by path; #10 lists four files with line numbers. An estimator handed that text is not predicting — it is transcribing. Handling, fixed now:

- The ticket goes to the estimator **as written**. That is realistic; real tickets do name files, and rewriting them would be a different experiment.
- Every item reports **two surprise sets**: the raw one, and the one restricted to files the ticket did not name. **The second is the honest signal.** For a ticket that names its targets, a good raw score is worth nothing.
- Where a ticket names files, that is recorded in its pre-registration so the discount is visible before results, not argued afterwards.

### Abandon gate

If after three items every surprise classifies as `E`, the briefing is too thin to support the measure and the protocol is revised or dropped rather than continued. Recorded now so continuing is a decision rather than momentum.

## Ordering discipline

The estimate is written to a pre-registration file **before** its results are read by me. Implementation may proceed in parallel — the estimator is independent — but I do not open the estimate until the implementation is complete and green, so the touch set cannot be steered toward it.

**This failed on the first run and the mechanism, not the intention, was at fault.** A background agent's full answer is delivered into my context automatically as a completion notification; there is no version of "I will not read it yet" that survives that. Item 05 is flagged contaminated as a result.

**Required from item 04 onward: the estimator writes its answer to a file under `reports/surprise/` and returns only a completion token.** The notification then carries nothing. This is the only form of the discipline that is actually enforceable, and it is worth stating plainly that the first version was enforceable only by my own good intentions — which is the same class of defect this ticket found five times in its measuring instruments.

## The review-cadence measure is a DIFFERENT measure, and its unit is not the file

Kept here because the two get conflated — this protocol measures *whether the change surface was foreseeable*; the cadence trigger measures *whether a human can still review what has accumulated*. Same series of items feeds both, but they are not the same number.

**Arnon's position, stated when the cadence idea was first discussed and restated 2026-08-08: what matters is the number of locations carrying meaningful change, the size of those changes, and the cognitive complexity — both of the change and of the resulting code.** Not files touched.

Item 05 is the evidence for why the distinction is load-bearing. It touched 26 files, which a file-count trigger would call large. Arnon reviewed it and called it easy, in his words *"because it's mostly 1-line or docstring changes with actual changes concentrated in a handful of test files"*. **12 of the 26 changed by a single line.** A trigger on file count would have fired on a change that was genuinely cheap to review, and a threshold calibrated on such firings would be calibrated to noise.

What this project already has to build it from:

- **Meaningful-change locations**: the prose-only classifier (`prose_only_check.py`, AST comparison with docstrings stripped) already separates prose from code for free, and a line-delta threshold per file separates one-liners from substance. Both cheap.
- **Cognitive complexity of the result**: `pyscn` already computes it per function and runs in this repo. Half the measure exists.
- **Cognitive complexity of the change**: **judged low / medium / high** — Arnon's call, 2026-08-08, and it settles a question I was over-thinking. A rough ordinal is sufficient; what must not happen is faking it with a computed proxy, since this ticket's abandoned scorers were exactly plausible proxies that turned out anti-correlated with what they claimed to measure. An honest three-point judgement beats a precise wrong number.

### How the ordinal is taken, so it stays comparable across items

**Two independent ratings per item, both one word.**

1. **A blind judge, before Arnon reviews.** Sees the diff and the ticket, not my report, not my own rating. Pre-registered like everything else.
2. **Arnon, at review.** One word. This is the ground truth — his review capacity is the quantity the cadence trigger exists to protect, so his rating defines the scale rather than approximating it.

I do not rate. I made the change; my estimate of how hard it is to read is the least trustworthy one available.

**Disagreement between the two is the calibration signal**, and more informative than either alone: a change the judge calls `high` and Arnon calls `low` means the diff looks worse than it reads, which is precisely the case a mechanical trigger would over-fire on.

### The judge's guidance was wrong, and item 04 is the proof

**Arnon, 2026-08-09, on item 04:** *"Very easy to review. A bunch of trivial changes. All meaningful code changes in one module. In my book complexity is low. Not sure why it was rated high. From the get go, I would have expected low complexity just from the ticket text. Something is wrong with the way the blind upfront judgement is guided. This is probably at the root of why it over-estimates complexity all the time. Not the method - but the judgement, and that can probably be traced to the guidance."*

Judge said `high`; Arnon said `low`. That is the maximum possible disagreement on a three-point scale, on only the second item where both ratings exist.

**Diagnosis: the guidance asked for the wrong quantity.** The anchors below lead with *"changes a contract, an invariant, or control flow that other code depends on"*, and the judge duly enumerated four contract changes and rated `high`. But contract changes measure **how consequential a change is**, not **how expensive it is to read** — and the second is the thing the cadence trigger exists to protect. A change can move four contracts in four one-line edits.

What Arnon actually weighed: *most locations are trivial*, and *the substance is concentrated in one module*. The judge saw both and treated neither as decisive, because nothing told it to.

**Corrections to the judge's brief, effective item 15:**

1. **Classify every changed location as trivial/mechanical or substantive before rating.** Report both counts. A diff that is mostly trivial is `low` almost regardless of what the substantive minority does.
2. **Concentration dominates dispersion.** All meaningful change inside one module pulls hard toward `low`. The same volume spread across five modules is what makes something `medium` or `high`.
3. **Rate reading cost, explicitly not risk.** "This is a permission tool so getting it wrong is serious" is a statement about stakes and must not raise the rating. Say so in the brief, because it is the trap the judge fell into.
4. **"Several modules must be held together" is only `high` when a reviewer must hold them together to check a *substantive* location.** Needing context to confirm a one-line move is normal reading, not cognitive load.
5. **Sanity-check against the ticket.** Ask what the ticket text alone would predict, and justify any departure. Arnon's read of item 04 from the ticket alone was `low`, and that turned out to be the right answer — so the ticket is a usable prior, and a rating that diverges from it needs a reason.

**Recorded as a Q2 result, not a Q1 one.** The instrument is not producing a bad number because a blind rating cannot work; it is producing a bad number because it was told to measure consequence and asked about cost. That is a guidance defect, and it is fixable — which is precisely the distinction Arnon drew between the method and the judgement.

**Do not retro-fit item 04's rating.** The `high` stands in the record as what the old guidance produced. Item 15 is the first test of the new brief.

Rough anchors, to stop the scale sliding:

| rating | anchor |
|---|---|
| **low** | mechanical or local. Understanding any one location does not require holding another in your head. Item 05 — Arnon's verdict was that it was easy to review because the substance sat in a handful of files and the rest were one-liners. |
| **medium** | a new idea to absorb, or several locations that must be understood together, but the shape is familiar. |
| **high** | changes a contract, an invariant, or control flow that other code depends on; or requires reconstructing why the old code was the way it was. |

**Item 05 is recorded `low` on Arnon's rating.** No blind-judge rating exists for it — the two-rating scheme starts at item 01.

**Refine as we learn** — Arnon's framing, and the reason no threshold is proposed here. The seven punch-list items produce the data; the threshold comes after.

## Measures dropped, and one added

**Dropped 2026-08-09, both on Arnon's acceptance:**

- **`|A|/|P|`** — see the scoring table. Wrong in both directions across four items, and inversely ranked against recall at the extremes every time.
- **Modified co-change `n/(n-1)`** — it is `1 + 1/(n-1)` by construction. Series: 05 `1.04` (n=26), 01 `1.07` (n=15), 04 `1.077` (n=14), 15 `1.10` (n=11). It has never carried information the file count did not, and it never can.

**Headline is now recall, with precision reported alongside**, split production/test and discounted for ticket-named files.

**Added, and not yet measured: scope size as a separate variable.** Arnon on item 15: *"Review was easy for the same reasons as the previous ones. Small scope, focused change helps that too."* The complexity anchors describe a change's *shape* and say nothing about its *size*. Every `low` so far has been on a small item, so shape and size are confounded in the data collected to date. Record raw size (locations, and lines excluding pure test additions) next to every rating from here, so the two can be separated. If size alone predicts the rating, the cadence trigger is far simpler than this protocol assumes — and that would be a genuine finding, not a defeat.

**On overshoots**, proposed at item 04 and still awaiting a decision: a category for "the ticket invited it, the implementer declined it", so a scope decision is not scored as estimator error. On item 04 it would have moved precision from 52% to 69%.

## CORRECTION — what this measure is actually for (Arnon, 2026-08-09)

**I had the criterion wrong throughout, and every "finding" scored against actual needs re-reading in this light.**

> The surprise metric is not supposed to predict the actual. It is supposed to align with **my** expectations. It is accurate if the preliminary expectation of what would change and how complex it is matches what I would have thought. So the precision/recall you measure is not useless — but it is not a criterion for the utility of the measure.

**Only his testimony validates the estimate.** Before or after is fine — *"I don't care that the after is sort of contaminated. I can distinguish well between my expectation before and my learning after. I am not mechanical."* Recall against the actual touch set is a property of the world, not of the instrument.

### The two things this measure is for

1. **An initial impression of the size of the ask** — enough to chop it up or scale it back before starting. *"Everybody's predictions about this are of known poor quality, so take it with a spoonful of salt. It's just one more directional indicator."*
2. **Once the prediction aligns with his prior, a surprise becomes a trigger to ask WHY.** A surprise in either direction is not a failure of the metric. The typical one — it was more than expected — is a signal to look deeper:
   - Is the code a mess, so the blast radius was larger than it should have been?
   - Did we find dormant bugs that were there all along?
   - Did the work surface a **misalignment between the code's conceptual model and reality**?

   Answering that is what produces follow-up decisions that improve quality. **The metrics are indicators pointing at improvement, not a prediction game.** They earn their place if they push toward better quality often enough, and fail if they send us on wild goose chases.

**Timescale: months, not days.** In days we can disqualify some, invent new ones, and get a clue about tuning. Nothing here is provable yet.

### The failure this exercise has NOT caught, which is itself a finding

> In almost every case in TOO-45 new problems worth looking at were discovered. At the same time the metrics failed to automatically flag your apparent inability to follow industry architectural good practices — even those specifically named in this very session several times over.

Recorded plainly, with the instances: scoping `hook.py` out of #04, deferring `migrate()`'s outcome type, leaving reporter state in undeclared globals, and shipping a tool registry that describes built-ins while a user-declared tool gets a name and nothing else. **Every one was caught by Arnon, with a single question. None by any metric, blind agent, or test.**

Two conclusions, and the second matters more:

- No good metric for this has been found. That is a reason to keep trying new ones, not to stop.
- **Manual review is the control that works for architectural error, and therefore ease of manual review is a first-class goal** — not a nicety. Bugs, by contrast, *are* being caught by process improvements (blinded agents, multiple review perspectives). The two failure classes have different detectors, and only one of them is automatable so far.

## Whatever survives has to be TRACKED, not just concluded

**Arnon, 2026-08-09**, and it changes what "finishing" this experiment means:

> I'd rather develop a disciplined way to calibrate estimations over time as we collect hard evidence of the pattern of deviation from estimate in retrospect. Whatever metrics and estimation methods survive will have to be tracked over time (exactly how TBD).

So the deliverable is **not** a verdict on which measures work. It is a measure plus **a mechanism that keeps measuring it**, because the value is in the drift over time, not in any single reading.

His framing on the recall decline is the reason. Recall fell as items grew on both #01 and #10, and his response was not "the estimator is bad" but that **under-estimation is the normal condition of software development** — notorious enough that some methodologies stopped estimating altogether. His preference is the harder path: keep estimating, and **calibrate against the observed deviation pattern in retrospect**.

That reframes the whole instrument. A recall of 63% is not a failing grade; it is one observation of a bias whose *stability* is the thing worth knowing. A bias with a stable sign and magnitude is correctable by a multiplier. An unstable one is not, and that is what would actually condemn the method.

**Consequences to design for, now rather than at the end:**

1. **Per-item records must stay machine-readable enough to aggregate.** Today they are prose with tables. Whatever tracking mechanism gets built will need the numbers extractable — recall, precision, cause counts, both complexity ratings, size.
2. **Record the deviation, not only the score.** The interesting series is estimate-vs-actual per item over time, which is what a calibration multiplier would be fitted to.
3. **Ratings need both raters every time.** The judge/owner pair is the calibration signal; a missing owner rating breaks that item for this purpose.
4. **Size is now a recorded variable** (see above), precisely so it can be tested as a competing explanation rather than assumed away.
5. **The mechanism itself is TBD and is its own piece of work.** Do not let it default to "I will remember to write the note" — that is the failure mode this project has documented repeatedly.

**Arnon's own rating is the ground truth and arrives at manual review.** His expectation-versus-actual on each ticket is part of the data, not commentary on it.

## The experiment answers TWO questions, not one

**Arnon, 2026-08-09**, and it reframes everything above:

> About that experiment we need to answer two separate questions: (1) in the state we've been experimenting with — does it produce a good signal? (2) is it structured well, and if so can it be tuned to produce a better signal?

> Remember that a big part of it is judgement. So even though you found so far that it errors consistently on one side — with a very low sample size — it can be tuned easily towards the centre of the distribution once we learn enough. So even if after enough samples it does not produce a usable signal, by tuning the judgement side we can nudge it back towards usefulness.

**Why this matters to how results are reported.** The findings so far — the ratio wrong in both directions, recall inversely ranked against it — answer Q1 only. I had been treating them as if they answered Q2, and they do not. A biased instrument with a sound structure is a *tuning* problem; an instrument whose structure cannot carry the signal is a *design* problem. Two different verdicts, and only the second justifies abandoning anything.

Concretely, from here:

- **Every finding is filed under Q1 or Q2.** "The ratio flatters short predictions" is Q1 evidence about the current calibration. "Section 4 outperformed sections 1-3" is Q2 evidence about structure — and it is *positive*, which the Q1 numbers alone completely obscure.
- **Consistent one-sided error is a tuning input, not a failure.** Bias with a stable sign is the easiest defect an instrument can have. What would condemn the design is error with no stable sign, or error uncorrelated with anything actionable.
- **The judgement half is the tuning surface.** The briefing contents, the cause categories, the sealed/open split, the complexity anchors — these are all adjustable, and adjusting them is the intended response to a poor Q1 result, not a workaround for one.
- **The abandon gate is a Q2 gate, not a Q1 gate.** It fires only if the *structure* cannot carry signal. Three all-`E` items would mean the briefing cannot distinguish instrument noise from architecture, which is structural. A merely biased ratio never fires it.

**Sample size is the binding constraint on Q1 and nothing can shortcut it.** Two items decided nothing; seven will decide little more. Arnon's own position: *"no single example will make a statistic. But 10 examples do — and we'll have them, and more."* So Q1 stays open by default, and any Q1 claim states its `n` in the same sentence.

### Known imperfection in the sealed/open split, recorded at item 04

The uncertainties half is supposed to name mechanisms and searches, never files. In practice its suggested searches *cite* files by path (`grep ... toolguard/error_log.py`), which tells the implementer those files are on the estimator's mind. The leak is partial and mild — the cited files were derivable from the ticket — but it is real and it is inherent to "give me a runnable search". Recorded rather than fixed: forbidding paths in the searches would gut the half that has proven most valuable. From here the raw score is reported alongside a note of which files leaked through the open half.

## Per-item record

One file per item under `reports/surprise/`, holding the pre-registration, the actual touch set, the scored comparison, and the cause assignment.
