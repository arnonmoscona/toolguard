# TOO-45 compound/resolve cycle -- blind judgment

Read: both plans in full, `toolguard/compound.py` (1,248 lines), `toolguard/resolve.py`, `toolguard/permission_resolution.py`, `toolguard/config_types.py` (verdict types), `toolguard/parser/command_extractor.py` (`_apply_leaf_policy`), `tools/architecture_fitness.py` (R1 predicates), `docs/architecture.md`, and the relevant test modules. No other report in this directory was opened.

## 1. Verdict

**Plan B, with four refinements (section 6). Not the fallback.**

Plan B is simpler on the one criterion that matters, and it is simpler for a reason that survives inspection rather than presentation: **its decomposition maps one-to-one onto the two altitudes the code already has.** Today the logic is a per-unit function (`_resolve_leaf_detailed`, plus the `UndecidableSegment` branch that should have lived beside it) and a cross-unit combinator (`_combine_strictest`), driven by a walk. Plan B names exactly those three things -- `judge_unit`, `_combine_strictest`, the driver -- and changes only what crosses between them. Plan A merges the two altitudes into one function called `combine` that applies per-unit policy *and* runs the cross-unit combination *and* owns `sub_matches`, and adds a pairing type to carry the plan and the verdicts together. Plan A removes the cycle; it does not meaningfully reduce what a maintainer must hold in their head, because it trades three callbacks for one large multi-altitude function plus two new types plus a new field on a shared audit dataclass.

Plan B's central move is the one I would have asked for independently: **make "which strings must be decided" a field on the decomposition result.** That is the entire content of the callback. Once `unit.parts` exists, the caller resolves and hands the results in, and every branch of the floor reads `part_verdicts[0]` where it read `probe(outer_cmd)`. I verified this is exact: `_resolve_leaf_detailed` calls `probe()` exactly once, at line 370, and none of the five ask-floor branches resolves anything again. The callback is not load-bearing for the floor; it is load-bearing only for the *timing* of one call.

Plan B also finds the free win Plan A defers. `_combine_strictest`'s 5-tuple `(decision, reason, leaf_text, context, fallback_kind)` is field-for-field `UnitVerdict.(decision, reason, sub_command, additional_context, fallback_kind)` -- I checked every construction site. Converting it (Plan B step 1) collapses two parallel constructions in `resolve_compound_permission_detailed`: the `UndecidableSegment` branch currently builds an `all_results` 5-tuple **and** a separate `UnitVerdict` for `record_unit` from the same seven values (compound.py:1134 and :1150), and the `LeafCommand` branch unpacks a `UnitVerdict` into a 5-tuple only to have `_combine_strictest` re-read the same fields (compound.py:1168-1176). Both become `all_results.append(outcome)`. Plan A puts this at **S5b, "optional, only if S3 came in short"** -- which means Plan A's `combine` juggles quads internally for the whole of the work, and the largest single mechanical simplification available in this file is the first thing dropped if the schedule slips. That ordering choice is, by itself, strong evidence about which author had the clearer picture of where the cost lives.

Summing the design column: **~4-5 hours of the 7-9 hour gap is real cost that Plan A's design imposes and Plan B's does not.** The remaining ~2 hours is Plan B under-budgeting the docstring sweep and skipping the evidence step. So the honest converged figures are approximately **Plan A 11-13 h (accurate), Plan B 5-7 h (its 3-5 is 1-2 h light).** Even after correcting Plan B upward, the gap is roughly 2x, and every hour of it is attributable to a specific design choice I can name. That is exactly the signal the owner's heuristic is looking for, and it points at Plan B.

One caution on the direction of the heuristic: "long implementation implies wrong design" is being applied here to two plans for the *same* problem, which is the case where it is most reliable. It should not be used to conclude that Plan B's 5-7 h is itself suspicious -- the honest floor for this change is roughly "one characterization test + one type + two function extractions + a driver + 44 doc references", and 5-7 h is a fair price for that.

## 3. Complexity: what a maintainer holds afterwards

Counting concepts, not lines. A "concept" here is something you cannot skip and cannot learn from the function in front of you.

**Today (10):** (1) the walk over `extract_structured`; (2) `isinstance` dispatch over two parser types; (3) the second, PEG split inside a leaf; (4) `resolve_one` -- a lossy 3-tuple **that also secretly appends to two of the caller's lists**; (5) `resolve_outer` -- a 5-tuple probe that must *not* have that side effect, existing only because of (4); (6) `record_unit` -- a third callback that writes the caller's list from inside the callee; (7) `_combine_strictest` consumed at two altitudes with two different meanings of `leaf_text`; (8) `fallback_kind` computed structurally in `resolve.py` and textually in `compound.py` for the same sub-command; (9) the emergent, undocumented-anywhere-single-place invariant that `sub_matches` ends up correct only because (4), (5) and (6) interlock; (10) a runtime cycle no import graph shows. Concepts 4-6, 9 and 10 are unreadable without holding both files open at once.

**After Plan B (7):** (1) `decompose -> [CommandUnit]`, four kinds, pure; (2) `parts` = "strings a rule engine must decide on this unit's behalf"; (3) `judge_unit(unit, part_verdicts, fallback) -> UnitVerdict`, pure, owns both floors; (4) `_combine_strictest([UnitVerdict]) -> RuntimeVerdict`; (5) the twelve-line driver in `resolve.py`, including the recording rule; (6) the positional invariant `part_verdicts` matches `unit.parts`; (7) the legacy 3-tuple adapter for the off-live-path API. Every one is legible from the function in front of you. Nothing is a callback; nothing is a side channel. Concepts 1, 3 and 4 are independently unit-testable with literals -- today none of them is testable without authoring a fake resolver.

**After Plan A (9):** (1) `decompose -> [PlannedUnit]`; (2) `ResolvedUnit`, a type whose only job is to pair two things (Plan A says so itself); (3) `combine`, one function owning per-unit policy, cross-unit combination, *and* `sub_matches` construction -- the largest single function in the file afterwards, and the only place where both altitudes are simultaneously in play; (4) `_combine_strictest` still on 5-tuples unless S5b ran; (5) `UnitVerdict.override` with a conditional-population contract; (6) `overrides` as a derived projection over `sub_matches`; (7) the driver comprehension; (8) the four-function legacy adapter family with `fallback_kind_for_reason` confined to it; (9) the plan/verdict positional invariant, same as Plan B's. Net: the cycle is gone and the callbacks are gone, but concept count falls from 10 to 9 and the single biggest function in the module gets bigger. That is not the outcome the exercise was for.

Plan A's genuine advantage, stated fairly: `sub_matches` is owned by `compound`, so the caller never needs to know the recording rule. Plan B pushes `if unit.kind == "plain": extend(parts) else: append(judged)` into `resolve.py`. Refinement R1 below closes most of that gap without adopting Plan A's shape.

## 4. Strongest argument against my own choice

**Plan B relocates the exact defect class R1e was created to fix, and does not fully close it.**

R1e existed because an audit entry's *existence* depended on a code path (an `UndecidableSegment` never called `resolve_one`, so it never reached `sub_matches`, so 813 of 975 compound-allow corpus cases under-logged). Plan B's driver decides audit entries by a `kind` string comparison **in a different module from where kinds are defined**. Add a fifth kind in `compound.decompose` and `resolve.py` silently routes it to the `else` branch. Nothing fails; the suite stays green; the audit trail is quietly wrong for the new kind. That is the same failure signature as R1e, one level up. Plan A's `combine` structurally cannot have this failure, because the branch that produces the verdict and the line that records it are adjacent -- and Plan A's `commands = ()` framing for `UndecidableSegment` is genuinely the more elegant statement of why the R1e case is not special. If I weight "the defect this ticket already paid for must not be able to recur" above "fewest concepts", Plan A wins.

I do not weight it that way, for two reasons. First, the brief's criterion is comprehensibility, and Plan A buys that structural guarantee by merging two altitudes into one function -- paying in the currency the exercise is measured in. Second, the guarantee is recoverable in Plan B for about ten minutes of work: put the recording rule on `CommandUnit` as data (refinement R1), and the rule lives with the kinds again while the `append`/`extend` stays visible in the driver. A structural guarantee obtainable by one boolean field is not a reason to accept a design that is otherwise more expensive by 4-5 hours.

Secondary counter-argument, weaker but real: Plan B's estimate is its own advertisement. "3-5 hours" reads as a claim about the design, and I have found it 1-2 hours light. An implementer who takes it literally will feel behind at hour five and start cutting -- and the first thing that gets cut is step 6, the docstring sweep, which Plan A correctly identifies as non-optional. Refinement R4 addresses this.

## 5. Independent check of the ASK-floor reasoning

Both plans conclude the floor stays in `compound`. **The conclusion is right. One of the two reasons both give is materially overstated, and both miss a third reason that is stronger than the one they overstate.**

**Claim: "the floor is triggered by parser facts, not rule facts." TRUE.** `LeafCommand.ask_floor` is set in `command_extractor._apply_leaf_policy` (command_extractor.py:573, setting `ask_floor=True` at :603 and :609); `UndecidableSegment` is produced by the parser layer. `compound` is the only module that reads either. Plan A's attribution of `_apply_leaf_policy` by name is correct.

**Claim: "the floor needs the RESULT of resolving the stub, not the ABILITY to resolve." TRUE, and this is the load-bearing one.** `_resolve_leaf_detailed` calls `probe(outer_cmd)` exactly once (compound.py:369-372) and no branch below re-resolves. All five ask-floor branches consume only `(decision, reason, additional_context, matched_rule, provenance)`. So the injected callable is not required by the floor at all -- it is required only because the walk resolves lazily. Both plans state this; Plan B states it most sharply.

**Claim: "moving the floor to `resolve.py` would silently unfloor `check_compound_permission` -- a fail-open security regression." OVERSTATED, in both plans.** `check_compound_permission` has **zero** production callers (grep over `toolguard/` and `tools/` returns only its own definition and docstring mentions), and `docs/architecture.md:244` documents it explicitly: *"retained but off the live path... the runtime hook no longer uses it."* It cannot fail open in production because nothing in production calls it. Plan B's "roughly forty tests... would silently lose the floor (a fail-open security regression)" and Plan A's "one is a security regression" both dress an API-contract-and-test-churn problem as a security one. The valid residue is still sufficient: moving the floor would either duplicate it -- and TOO-45 D4 already fixed exactly that duplication, per the comment at compound.py:1094-1096 -- or leave a public, 46-times-referenced API silently unfloored. Keep the conclusion; discard the framing. This matters beyond pedantry: a security argument that does not survive checking teaches the next reader to trust the *next* one without checking.

**A third reason neither plan gives with full force.** The floor's six reason wordings are *data* consumed by `_FALLBACK_REASON_MARKERS` (compound.py:202-208) and by `fallback_kind_for_reason`, which `hook.py` calls to choose between a real rule name and `FALLBACK_DENY_PLACEHOLDER`/`FALLBACK_ALLOW_PLACEHOLDER`. Splitting the code that *writes* those strings from the table that *recognises* them across a module boundary would make the marker table's own drift warning come true. Plan A gestures at this (its reason 2); Plan B does not mention it, and it is stronger than the `check_compound_permission` argument Plan B leads with.

**Security posture after the change: improved, in both plans.** The floor becomes a pure function of `(unit, part_verdicts, fallback)`, testable with literals. Today it is unreachable in a test without constructing a callable, which is why the twelve-cell strictness table in `_apply_undecidable_floor`'s docstring is documented but, as far as I can tell, not exhaustively pinned. Both plans correctly identify the one genuine fail-open risk -- `decompose` mis-mapping `ask_floor=True` to `kind="plain"`, which would let `python -c "<anything>"` be allowed by a `python *` rule -- and both correctly insist `parts` carries the **untruncated** stub (verified: `_extract_outer_command` deliberately does not truncate, compound.py:625-629; `TestExtractOuterCommandDenyStillFires` at test_compound.py:1861 pins it, exactly as Plan B claims).

**Both plans are right to leave `apply_parse_failure_floor`'s second application alone** (resolve.py:809-826). Verified: it is the only floor covering a command line made entirely of undecidable segments, and its own comment says so at length. Plan B's R6 and Plan A's implicit "nothing else in resolve.py changes shape" both hold.

## 7. Refinement instructions for Plan B

**R1 (the single biggest one -- makes the R1e defect class structurally impossible again).** Do not put `if unit.kind == "plain"` in `resolve.py`. Put the recording rule on `CommandUnit` as data, set by `decompose`:

```python
audits_as_one: bool   # True for 'inline_code' and 'undecidable': the FLOOR decided,
                      # not any part's rule match, so the unit is ONE audit entry.
                      # False for 'plain' and 'unknown': the audit entries ARE the
                      # unit's own parts (a zero-part unit records nothing).
```

The driver then reads `sub_matches.append(judged) if unit.audits_as_one else sub_matches.extend(part_verdicts)` -- the append stays visible in `resolve.py`, the *rule* lives beside the kinds that motivate it, and a fifth kind cannot be added without deciding this field. As a bonus it deletes Plan B's one acknowledged behaviour change (B5): `unknown` gets `audits_as_one=False` with `parts=()`, so it records nothing, exactly as today. Byte-identical becomes byte-identical with no asterisk.

**R2. Keep step 1 (`_combine_strictest` -> `List[UnitVerdict]`) first and mandatory, and make its payoff explicit in the coder brief**, because it is the step most likely to be skipped as "just a type change". State the two collapses it enables so the implementer recognises them when they arrive: the `UndecidableSegment` branch's duplicate construction (compound.py:1134 + :1150 built from the same seven values) becomes one object, and the `LeafCommand` branch's unpack-and-repack (compound.py:1168-1176) becomes `all_results.append(outcome)`.

**R3. Relocate the floor branches verbatim; forbid tidying them.** The five ask-floor `UnitVerdict` constructions in `_resolve_leaf_detailed` are visibly near-duplicate and will tempt a table-driven rewrite. They must not be rewritten in this change -- this is the one branch in the codebase where a silent inversion is a security hole. Add Plan A's twelve-cell test matrix over {stub decision} x {`ask`, `deny`, `allow_with_warning`, `allow`} against `_apply_undecidable_floor`'s documented table *before* moving anything, and Plan A's A4 test (an ask-floor stub that allows via a more-specific allow over a less-specific deny must produce **zero** entries in `RuntimeVerdict.overrides`). Both are cheap and neither is in Plan B.

**R4. Re-budget to 5-7 hours and make step 6 non-negotiable.** Take Plan A's docstring inventory verbatim -- it is measured and accurate where Plan B's is not: 44 references under `toolguard/` (compound.py 38, resolve.py 5, hook.py:495 1), plus `_resolve_leaf_detailed` at config_types.py 570/589/641/788 and `_resolve_leaf` at permission_resolution.py:124. Two of `compound.py`'s densest doc blocks (`ResolveOuterProbe`, `RecordUnitVerdict`, compound.py:32-56) are deletions, not rewrites. Also import Plan A's **abandon gate**, attached to Plan B's step 3: *if extracting `judge_unit` needs anything beyond mechanical relocation -- a new conditional keyed on `kind` past the four that exist, or a branch that wants a second look at a resolver -- stop, revert to the end of step 2, and ship the documented fallback.* Plan B is abandonable at every boundary but never says what to do instead; a gate with no named destination is not a gate.

**R5 (minor).** `judge_unit` should `raise` on an unrecognised `kind` rather than falling through, and should raise `ValueError` on a `len(part_verdicts) != len(unit.parts)` mismatch, both with tests -- Plan B proposes the second, not the first. Add `_unit_from_tuple`'s own unit test (Plan B's own "what gets harder" item 5, which it is right to flag). Drop the `--strict-prose` claim to a plain instruction: any prose diff is a defect until proven otherwise, and goldens are never regenerated to make this refactor pass.

## 8. Why not the fallback

I considered it seriously and reject it, on Plan A's argument rather than Plan B's -- Plan A makes the better case against its own escape hatch.

The fallback is not the cheap option it looks like. Typing the strategy `Callable[[str], UnitVerdict]` requires the same `UnitVerdict`-crossing-the-boundary contract, the same adapter for the 3-tuple test closures, and the same relocation of `sub_matches` accumulation onto the returned verdict. Once those three are done, `resolve_outer` and `record_unit` have both already become unnecessary and the injected callable is the only remaining thing holding the cycle up -- so the fallback costs perhaps 60-70% of Plan B's price and stops one step short of the finish. What it buys for that price is a `compound.py` that still cannot be read without following a callback into `resolve.py`, documented under a name -- "strategy pattern" -- that would be accurate only after `record_unit` is gone. Plan B is right that naming it that while a second callback still mutates the caller's list is worse than documenting nothing, because a reader who trusts the name stops looking.

The fallback remains the correct landing spot if step 3 goes badly, which is precisely why R4 attaches Plan A's abandon gate to it. Taking it *first*, though, would be paying most of the fare to get off one stop early.
