# Working methods with evidence behind them — extracted from TOO-45

Extracted 2026-08-23 so the TOO-45 notes can be deleted. **The organising rule: a practice is listed only where something was measured, or where the same practice produced the same kind of result across independent instances.** Where the only support is an assertion, it says so. Sections 4 and 5 exist because a list of successes is a marketing document — several things in the brief's candidate list came out weaker than their reputation, and two came out refuted.

Every claim cites the file it came from. Paths are relative to `toolguard-memories/`.

**Read the cost lines with care (added 2026-08-23, from the verification pass).** Of the fifteen practices priced here, only **three** carry a re-measurable cost — §1.1, §1.2 and §4.1's blinded pass. Two more (§2.1, §2.3) state honestly that no cost was recorded. **The rest recommend a practice with no price attached at all**: §1.7, §1.8, §2.2, §4.2 and §4.3 have no cost line, §1.5's is a shape rather than a cost, and §1.9 offers round counts, which are not cost. §2.3 names the reason in its own words — *"none of the three traces records its own wall-clock or token cost, so the return on investment is qualitative"* — and that admission applies far more widely than the section it sits in.

**One framing to carry, because it explains most of what follows.** TOO-45's practices divide into three jobs that are constantly confused: **direction** (is the codebase getting better?), **acceptance** (did this step do what it claimed?), and **diagnosis** (what is actually happening in here?). The ticket produced a clean proof they cannot be the same instrument — the best directional metric on the project, co-change, is *actively destroyed* by the refactor it would be used to judge (71 → 134 fully-coupled pairs from one 23-file ticket, `TOO-45/reports/retrospective.md` §3). **Corrected 2026-08-23 — the qualifier belongs with the number:** that 71 → 134 is under *ticket* grouping. Under **per-commit** grouping the same change moved only **39 → 42**, and the source (`retrospective.md` §3.3) attributes the distortion to a **7x sample-size loss** caused by grouping at ticket level. The indictment of co-change as a directional metric stands; the size of the effect is grouping-dependent, and the flat version of the sentence overstates it. **The diagnostic class produced the most findings per unit cost and was the least planned for.** Nearly every strong practice below is diagnostic.

## 1. Strongest evidence — measured, and repeated across independent instances

### 1.1 Execute the claim instead of reading it

**What it is.** Treat every sentence about behaviour — a docstring, a ticket's justification, a brief, your own note — as a claim to be run, not read. Three to twenty lines that import the real module, feed it the case the sentence is about, and print what came back.

**Evidence.** The strongest single body in the whole campaign, and it arrives from four independent directions:

| instance | number | source |
|---|---|---|
| A comment sweep whose goal was *shortening prose* filed **16 defect tickets it was not looking for**, several against the permission engine | 4 of the first 6 open with near-identical sentences, written by different agents on different days: found "by executing a docstring claim rather than reading it" | `TOO-45/reports/transferable-practices.md` |
| Brute force over a tiny alphabet against the `[native]` wildcard matcher | 7,623 pattern/command pairs, **416 mismatches across 46 patterns, all false negatives**, all on patterns not ending in `*` — the shape the project's own docs recommend. 15 existing tests pass even with the matching branch rebound to the wrong implementation | `TOO-45/reports/transferable-practices-evidence.md` §D |
| Reading vs. mutation on the same module | reading pass reported "one minor redundancy"; mutation found **13 of 25 mechanisms at zero detection**. On another module reading found nothing and mutation found five | `methodology/in-process-mutation-testing.md` |
| Arnon's corrections corpus | "execution is king" is **13 occurrences, the single most recurrent theme** across 210 human turns; every correction that mattered came from something that ran | `TOO-45/reports/corrections-analysis.md` |

**The mechanism, which is the transferable part.** A docstring is close to the only place intent is stated in *universally quantified* form and nothing downstream ever checks it. Tests check inputs someone thought of; types check shapes; a comment states a law and by construction nothing runs it.

**Cost, measured.** ~100k tokens per verification review round; five rounds on one ticket; individual repair passes ~27 min / ~$2.25 and ~40 min / ~$4-6, plus a blinded review pass at **~10 min / ~$3**. The source's summary sentence, quoted whole rather than trimmed: *"Call it a few hundred dollars ... and **understand that** most of the spend buys sentences nobody will read."* (`methodology/verifying-claims-finds-bugs.md`) **Corrected 2026-08-23:** the earlier version of this line quoted that sentence with quotation marks while cutting it mid-sentence, and dropped the ~10 min / ~$3 blinded-pass data point.

**When it is NOT worth doing — this is stated in the source, not inferred.** Prose on code whose behaviour is cheap to re-derive. Modules where the honest answer is to delete most comments unread. Claims about concurrency, failure ordering, or another process's behaviour resist the method — delete the sentence rather than write a longer probe. **"If the budget only covers one pass, spend it deleting, not verifying."** And it only finds defects *where prose exists*: an undocumented mechanism is invisible to it, which is exactly where mutation pays — the two campaigns ran over overlapping code and their findings barely intersect.

### 1.2 Mutation testing as a discovery instrument, not only a gate

**Confirmed, and it is the best-supported single practice on the ticket.** Three distinct discovery modes, each measured:

- **A mutation that refuses to change behaviour is pointing at a second implementation.** Two of five seeded mutations came back MISSED; the corpus was briefly blamed and was fine — each mutation hit one of *two independent implementations of the same idea*. That is how the duplicated undecidable floor was found, before any refactoring started. After unification, **the identical mutation flipped MISSED → CAUGHT**, which is a falsifiable proof that a unification is real rather than cosmetic. (`TOO-45/TOO-45 lessons.md` §7, §10; `retrospective.md` §4.4)
- **Mutate toward the fix, not only away from correctness.** For a TOML escaping bug: removing *all* escaping produced **16 failures** (looks like coverage); applying the *actual fix* produced **0 failures**. "The suite is blind in both directions" is strictly stronger than "untested". (`transferable-practices-evidence.md` §E)
- **Mutate what you just built, not only what you are protecting.** Mutating a new field to a plausible *wrong* value left **all 2,300 tests green**; mutating it to `None` failed 2. The suite pinned absence, not correctness. Swapping `matched_rule` and `provenance` at the hook call site corrupts every audit entry and **2,314 tests stayed green**. (`TOO-45 lessons.md` §13 for the 2,300-green mutation; **corrected 2026-08-23** — the 2,314-green swap is a *separate* measurement and is not in §13, it is at `retrospective.md:211` and `TOO-45 decision log.md:731`.)

**Scale evidence.** Per-module survival before repair: **47% (14/30), 55% (23/42), 58% (over 81 mutants)**, near zero after repair. Roughly **fifty production defect tickets, several security-shaped, from mutations alone** — **corrected 2026-08-23**, the source records that fifty is *"a floor, not a ceiling"* and that only **1 of its 3 supporting batches was independently verified** (`transferable-practices-evidence.md:61`); quote it as a lower bound of partly-verified provenance, not as a count. The pattern that generalised hardest: *the mechanism a module is named for was usually its least-tested part*. (`methodology/in-process-mutation-testing.md`)

**It also works as an ordinary acceptance gate, and the null results are recorded honestly.** Item 95 ran 4 mutations one at a time, reverting between each, with byte-identical revert verified by `git diff --stat` plus `grep -c MUTATION`: failures came back 30 / 20 / **exactly 1** (the test the ticket had predicted) / **exactly 1**. *"Every mutation was caught by an existing test. No coverage gap found, so no new test was added."* Contrast: on R3 a **blinded reviewer ran 29 mutations against R3's final state and returned 5 new findings, all defects introduced by R3 itself** (`implementation/TOO-45 R3 second review-fix coder task recall.md:12`). **Corrected 2026-08-23:** the earlier "29 mutations and 6 survived, both gaps then fixed and re-verified" was wrong three ways — the battery produced 5 findings, not 6 survivors; the "6 mutations survive" figure is scoped to **finding #1 alone** (the unpinned production wiring); and **only 2 mutations were re-verified by hand** against the acceptance criterion, the report saying the other four were *"not independently re-verified by hand"*. The sentence's own "both gaps" gave it away, since six cannot be both. R3's *first* review-fix records that `matched_rule=matched_pattern` **passed all tests before exact-equality assertions were added**, a genuine escape closed by the technique.

**Cost, measured.** ~161k tokens per repair agent (range 142k–197k, n=12). **Expect several times a read-and-edit campaign at equal agent count** — these agents run the suite dozens of times per module.

**When it is NOT worth doing / how it lies.** A badly chosen mutant that happens to agree with the original on every fixture reports zero detection for a covered mechanism — confirm the mutant actually changes output first. **Never read failure counts; diff the failing test *sets*.** Four mutually contradictory mutations all failed the identical single test; the worst instance had 34 zero-detection mutants sharing one signature — "the same failing set for every mutant is a *worse* signal than zero failures, because a non-zero count reads as coverage." And read the tracebacks: the sweep's highest-severity finding (a fail-open in the crash logger) produced 9 failures, 3 in the hook's own test file — all three from an `OSError` breaking an `assertRaises(SystemExit)` before any assertion reached stdout. A naive count would have hidden the worst finding of the sweep behind an apparent pass.

### 1.3 Tell every subagent the brief is unverified, and to verify it

**Confirmed, and it is the cheapest item in this document.** The instruction is one paragraph: *"do not take my word for any of this — verify it yourself."*

**Evidence.** The coordinator's briefs carried a false claim **at a steady rate for the entire campaign — roughly thirty corrections**, including the same figure wrong three times. Two runs of consecutive counting: **"four consecutive, all caught by the agent, none by me"** and later **"seven consecutive, every one caught by the agent."** **Corrected 2026-08-23:** these were previously called *independent* runs; they are not. Both are the **same coordinator's self-observation in the same running note**, at `phase 3 resume.md:158` and `:209` — two moments, one observer. In the #07 sweep specifically, **fifteen-plus briefs contained mistakes editors caught and refused**, two of which had already reached filed tickets before being caught. (`TOO-45/TOO-45 phase 3 resume.md`; `TOO-45/TOO-45 campaign resume 2026-08-13.md`; `TOO-45/reports/transferable-practices-evidence.md` §G)

**The diagnosed cause matters more than the count.** *"A report is a session delta; a review measures HEAD."* The coordinator forwards agent reports as current state, so its briefs go stale in a systematic direction — too tidy, too confident about counts and sole-consumer claims.

**It has negative controls, which is what makes the count credible.** Three sampled reports explicitly record the opposite outcome — *"Nothing in the brief was false. Every one of B1-B7 reproduced exactly as described"* (review-78 round 1 repair); *"Nothing in the brief was false... This breaks the week's streak"* (ticket 77 phase 1); *"Nothing in the brief was found false"* (review-80 round 1). The agents were not simply manufacturing corrections to look diligent.

**Worked instances, all from `toolguard-memories/implementation/` and `TOO-45/reports/`:** ticket 98 chunk 3 — brief called `tools/mining.py` "the one real consumer" of a re-export; the coder grepped first, found `toolguard/compound.py` also imports it, and refused the move. Ticket 74 — brief claimed "there is already a RED test"; the coder ran it and got `Ran 4 tests in 0.001s / OK`, and the real defect was in a different function. Ticket 19 — **the brief's suggested *fix shape* was wrong**, and the coder implemented it as briefed before catching that it dropped an `ask_floor` on `python3 - <<'EOF' 2>/dev/null || true` (*"Caught this via the corpus replay, not by inspection"*). Reviewers did it too: review-18 round 6 found the brief's *"changed docstring/comment only, zero logic"* was false — *"correct change, wrong description. Flagging because a reviewer told 'zero logic' would not have opened the file."*

**The single most useful contrast in the record.** Ticket 74 and ticket 18 share the same cause (inherited staleness in the brief). Ticket 18 cost ~11 hours; ticket 74 cost one command. *"The difference was execution before work, not better ticket-reading."* **Corrected 2026-08-23 — name the currency.** "~11 hours" is well sourced (`surprise/18-scored.md:61` and five other places), but the wall-clock table at `punch-list-2026-08-20.md:295` lists **item 18 at 4h15m**. The corpus carries two incompatible cost currencies — agent runs and tokens on one side, wall-clock on the other — and this document previously quoted whichever was larger without saying which. The qualitative contrast (a long grind against a single command) survives either way; the number does not travel without its unit.

**When it is NOT worth doing.** No case found — but note what it does *not* cover: it catches false claims, not omissions. Every one of the ~30 corrections was a wrong statement; nothing in the record shows an agent catching something the brief simply failed to mention.

### 1.4 A second review pass over any edit loop, because the editor writes new falsehoods

**What it is.** editor pass → cold review → editor pass → … → narrow delta check. The reviewer reads cold, verifies every checkable claim (weighting claims that reach outside the file), and only then diffs for losses.

**Evidence, and it is the counterintuitive part.** *"Reviews are catching about as many defects the **editor newly wrote** as ones it carried through."* On the four files reviewed after the unattended run began, the reviewer found defects **the editor itself newly wrote on three of them**, and on the fourth **refuted the editor's headline finding outright**. Across **twelve** files, **every falsehood an editor newly introduced was a claim about what a mechanism *guarantees*, never about what it *does*** — which produced the standing rule "state what the code does and stop; do not state what that means." **A single-pass sweep would have shipped all of it.** (`TOO-45/TOO-45 punch-list 07 work queue.md:308`, verbatim.) **Corrected 2026-08-23 — transitive citation, 2.5x inflation.** This document previously said *"roughly thirty files"* while citing the work queue, which says **twelve**. "Roughly 30 files" exists in exactly one place, `TOO-45/reports/transferable-practices-evidence.md:79`, where it appears **inside quotation marks as a quote of the work queue** — the dossier misquoted its own source and this summary inherited the inflated number from the dossier rather than from the primary. A universal ("every falsehood") over twelve files is a materially weaker claim than the same universal over thirty.

**Why it is worse than stale prose:** new text reads as freshly checked.

**Convergence and stopping rules, measured.** Findings per round on two tickets: **14, 14, 7, 4, 0** and **12+, 3, 4**. Only a round that returns *nothing* is a reliable finish line — round 3 of the first sequence returned 7 after two rounds of 14. And: **a new false claim introduced by the previous repair means the loop is circling — delete the sentence, do not schedule a fourth rewording.** (`methodology/verifying-claims-finds-bugs.md`)

**Cost.** Steady state **2-3 editor passes and 2 reviews plus a delta check per file**; calibration files took **4-5 editor passes with 4 reviews each** (`work queue.md:385`). **Corrected 2026-08-23:** the previous "calibration files took 4-5" dropped the "4 reviews each", roughly halving the stated calibration cost in the one sentence whose job is to price the practice.

**When it is NOT worth doing.** Where the pass is *deleting* rather than *rewriting* — deleting an unverified claim costs one round and cannot introduce a new one. The second review exists to catch authored text; if nothing was authored, its yield collapses.

### 1.5 Implementing a small plausible requirement finds defects that reading does not

**Arnon's own headline takeaway from the ticket** (`TOO-45/reports/review-conclusions.md`), and the numbers support it — though *not* for the purpose it was designed for (see §4.3).

**Evidence.** Four small requirements, each implemented independently in the pre-refactor and post-refactor trees by blinded agents. **Four for four, each implementer surfaced a pre-existing product defect while not looking for one**: two disagreeing definitions of a project root; two hardcoded copies of a file-tools list in one file (found by *both* implementers, and **neither by following code — only by grepping literal tuples**); a module disagreeing with itself about a default format; a doc still describing superseded behaviour. **Every one sat in code already read by seven directed report agents, one blinded reviewer, `pyscn`, `ruff`, and 2,586 passing tests.** (`TOO-45/reports/canary-results.md`)

**The contrast Arnon drew:** high-coverage unit testing is necessary but guards regressions and rarely uncovers dormant bugs; randomized perturbation by blinded agents does the opposite. **"A well-understood way to conduct a fishing expedition."** He also notes it is *uniquely practical with agents and too labour-intensive to be worth doing manually*, which is why it is thin in the SDLC literature.

**Cost — not measured.** Two independent implementations per requirement, plus pre-registration, n=4 here. **Corrected 2026-08-23:** that is a *shape*, not a cost. No wall-clock and no token figure was ever recorded for this practice, so it is priced only by the number of agent runs it obviously requires. Anyone budgeting it is estimating, not quoting.

**When it is NOT worth doing.** As a *scoring* instrument comparing two trees — see §4.3, where it is genuinely weak. Keep it as a defect-finder; do not keep it as a metric.

### 1.6 Exhaustive sweeps with no attention gradient

**What it is.** File by file, including the boring files, rather than where suspicion points.

**Evidence.** The sharpest instance: a five-rule over-match **already live in shipped self-permission configuration** was found through the docstring of *an advisory analyzer that exists to warn about redundant rules and enforces nothing* — exactly the file a suspicion-driven review skips. (`transferable-practices.md`)

**The stronger, more general version of the same point** comes from the corrections corpus: **correction rate tracked reviewability, not code quality.** The heaviest architectural objections arrived at turns 357-359, near the end, after surviving seven directed report agents, a blinded reviewer, `pyscn`, `ruff` and 2,600 passing tests. Arnon named the mechanism himself: *"Now that changes are fewer files I start noticing things."* **A reviewer's detection rate is a function of change-set size and collapses toward zero above some threshold — and a review of a large diff still *reports success*, which is the dangerous part.** (`TOO-45/reports/corrections-analysis.md`)

**Cost.** The whole #07 sweep: 158 files, ~9,100 lines of prose removed, two days. Both the file count and the line count re-measure (`transferable-practices-evidence.md:15`). **Corrected 2026-08-23:** this line previously ended "roughly 90 agents" — a figure **§7 of this same document already disqualifies** as never cross-checked against a batch count. It is dropped here rather than repeated, because the agent count is precisely the number a reader would quote when pricing a sweep.

**When it is NOT worth doing — measured, and stated as plainly as the successes.** The `test/` tier — the largest single tier — **produced zero product-code defects**, and no filed ticket traces to it. **Corrected 2026-08-23, two numbers had been spliced:** **86** is `git diff --stat test/`'s *changed-file* count (`work queue.md:25`), while the tier itself is **88 files**; and the zero-product-defect reading was taken **mid-sweep at 62 of 88 files**, a hedge the dossier states explicitly at line 101 (*"~78 is the denominator ticket 31 uses for the assertions-that-cannot-fail count, not necessarily 'all files examined for product defects'"*). The conclusion survives; the flat "86 files ... zero" attached a file count from one instrument to a hedged partial measurement from another. Its yield was entirely test-suite quality (~65 assertions that cannot fail, ~50 mechanisms with no test detection). Real output, different output; conflating the two overstates what sweeping test code buys. Marginal yield near the end was explicitly questioned at the time: *"roughly one per two files. No new product tickets; this tier buys test-suite quality, not defects."*

### 1.7 A scope boundary that forbids fixing, to force documenting

**What it is.** Two rules barred the sweep from correcting what it found: *"strings are code, do not touch them even when wrong"* and *"do not rewrite a false Given/Then to match what the assertions actually check."*

**Evidence.** Both converted would-be silent fixes into written findings with reproductions. A redundancy detector's user-facing message string still tells a user "this rule is redundant and can be dropped" after the surrounding docstring was corrected to say the opposite — on record **precisely because the sweep was forbidden to touch strings**. On the second rule, **five different editors independently converged on refusing to launder a false Given/Then** without being told twice. The framing is explicit in the source: *"prohibiting the fix increases the yield."* (`transferable-practices-evidence.md` §C.4, §H)

**The reasoning that makes it general:** an accurate rewrite of a false test description leaves a green test that pins nothing, behind a description nobody will re-read closely again.

**When it is NOT worth doing.** When the fix is genuinely one line and nobody is downstream of the finding — the boundary buys a paper trail, which is worth something only if somebody reads the trail. Note the campaign's own failure here: the sweep's largest findings sat for days in a 2,200-line working-notes file annotated "needs a ticket, not this sweep", *"which read as a decision and functioned as a burial."*

### 1.8 Two-phase change for generated artifacts — the grammar first, the consuming Python second

**The sharpest single statistic in the whole corpus.** A census of `TOO-45/reports/review-*.md` finds **zero passes among the code-and-prose review rounds — and the only two PASSes in the entire corpus are the two `.peg`-only reviews run under the two-phase grammar procedure.** **Corrected 2026-08-23 — the census as previously stated ("~26 genuine review rounds", "~24 code-and-prose") does not reproduce.** Re-enumerated: **35 files**, of which 4 are coder repair/fix reports and 1 is `review-conclusions.md`, leaving **30 review rounds** — **2 PASS** (both `.peg`), **27 explicit FAIL**, and 1 with no verdict line but two false-assertion findings (`review-44-round4.md`). So the non-peg count is **28**, not ~24, and the corpus is **30**, not ~26. The claim's direction is confirmed and was in fact *understated*; what was wrong is that "~26" was presented as a count somebody had performed.

**What the reviewers actually did with the phase-1 artifact, and why the gate held.** `review-77-grammar-phase1.md`: regenerated the grammar with canopy into a scratch directory and `diff`ed against the committed generated parser — **0 lines** — *"the single worst outcome for this gate, a hand-edited 'generated' file, is ruled out, not assumed"*; then a differential over **23,594 distinct commands with `differing=0`** and parse failures unchanged at `506 → 506`. `review-77-grammar-phase1-delta.md`: the reviewer **built the rejected variant grammar and measured it** — **0 differences over 28,770 corpus commands and 88 adversarial cases**, parse failures `4349 → 4349`.

**Why it is not just a project convention.** The phase separation gives the reviewer an artifact that is *mechanically checkable end to end* — regenerate, diff, replay a corpus — where every other review round in the corpus asked a judge to decide whether a change was correct.

**Corrected 2026-08-23 — "with no judgement required" was the wrong lesson, and neither PASS was a clean pass.** `review-77-grammar-phase1.md` is a **PASS that raised four findings**: M1 at line 112 — *"`$(...)` in an assignment value, and `$(...)` as the command word, **both defeat the ticket's purpose**; a verified grammar fix exists and belongs in phase 1"*, with *"Decision needed, and it needs to be made now, not in phase 2"* — plus L1, L2 and L3. A **second** phase-1 round was needed (`review-77-grammar-phase1-delta.md`), whose own PASS records *"all four M1 bypasses are now recognised."* So the first phase-1 artifact **passed every mechanical check while leaving four bypasses open**, and what caught them was the reviewer's judgement, not the byte-diff. That is a better story about reviewing and a weaker one about mechanisation: the two-phase gate makes a *large part* of the review mechanical and thereby frees attention for the part that is not — it does not remove the judgement.

Reports also record correctly deciding the procedure **did not apply** (ticket 19; the `statement_bounds_containing` refactor) rather than cargo-culting it.

**When it is NOT worth doing.** Only where a generated artifact exists. And it does not immunise phase 2: ticket 101's brace-grammar attempt got through an isolated validation and then produced **19 unexpected failures** on the real tree, including `{ ls; }` decomposing to `['{ ls', '}']` — *"a real deny-bypass, not just a test-shape mismatch."* **Corrected 2026-08-23 — the previous sentence here ("the ticket stood down mid-task with zero net change shipped, which is the right outcome and also the cost") is refuted by the repository.** Commit **`03d922c`** (2026-08-22 16:02), *"TOO-45 Item 101 - a bare {} is a word, and brace groups still decompose"*, **shipped the grammar fix** (`unquoted_word` admits `{}` while `{`/`}` stay in `delimiter`) and added `test/unit/test_deny_penetrates_constructs.py`, a new regression suite covering a denied command across all 17 supported constructs. What stood down was **one coder run**, not the ticket: `implementation/TOO-45 ticket 101 bare-brace grammar fix - coder implementation report.md:11` says *"Status: STOOD DOWN mid-task at the coordinator's explicit instruction. No net changes shipped"*, and its step 7 records that the coder *"had independently converged on the same narrower fix they specify"* — the fix the commit contains. **So the real cost of phase 2's limits here is one aborted agent run inside a ticket that landed a correct fix and a new test file the same day**, not a ticket abandoned for nothing. This is a note read as an outcome where git records a different one.

### 1.9 On security-shaped changes, review to exhaustion — and expect the repair to be the defect

**Round counts, measured.** Ticket 18 ran to **round 6**; ticket 78 to **round 5**; ticket 79 to **round 4**; tickets 39 and 80 to **round 3**. Blocking findings per round did not decay toward zero — ticket 18 went 2, 2, 1, 3, 3, 2.

**The late rounds were not ceremony, for the security-shaped tickets.** Review-78 **round 5** found `bash -c 'dd if=~/.ssh/id_rsa'` walking past an absolute deny rule and returning `PRIVATE-KEY-MATERIAL`. Review-18 **round 6** found `Bash([regex]\bcurl\b)` silently parsing to `\x08curl\x08` — **the exact shape the project's own audit skill recommends emitting.**

**And here is the reason the rounds kept finding things, which is the transferable part: a large share of review yield is defects the *repair* created.** **Corrected 2026-08-23 — the previous wording, "roughly half ... independently measured twice, from opposite ends of the campaign", is the document's own construction and not a measurement.** Neither cited source is a proportion of review yield:

- The #07 sweep's sentence: *"Reviews are catching about as many defects the **editor newly wrote** as ones it carried through."* (`punch-list 07 work queue.md:352`.) It sits under a heading that says "measured", but **no count follows** — six file names, no numerator, no denominator — and it is about a **prose-comment sweep**, not security code review. It is also **the same sentence §1.4 above already uses**, so it is not independent of this document's other section.
- Ticket 79's post-mortem: *"Eleven agent runs, four review rounds, and **three security weakenings — each introduced by the fix for the previous one**."* Three is a count, not a ratio, and nothing in it says "half" of anything.
- **The two "concretely" instances below are from tickets 78 and 39, not 79** — they do not instantiate 79's three weakenings, and the earlier text read as though they did. Review-78 round 5 caught an ask→allow loosening that **round 4's own repair had just introduced** on `echo hi >~/notes.txt`; punch-list 39 round 4 caught a `ConfigWriteVerificationError` regression the round's naive fix created, found only by running the full suite.

**The phenomenon is real and well evidenced across several tickets. The quantification was not.** Carry the mechanism — repairs introduce defects, so late rounds keep paying — and do not carry a fraction.

**When it is NOT worth doing.** The findings migrate from code to prose as rounds go on, and that is the signal to stop. Review-18 round 2: *"The production code is correct and native-faithful. Both blocking findings are about a claim and a missing test, not about the matcher."* Review-74 round 1 returned **five blocking findings and zero code defects** — *"Every blocking finding below is a false or overclaimed sentence... Four of the five are claims about measurements that were not measured."* Rounds 4-6 of ticket 44 and 1-3 of ticket 80 were prose-only by construction. **Track what kind of finding a round returns, not how many.**

**A round count is not a difficulty measure.** The campaign flagged this itself: ticket 19 ran one review round and *two* repair rounds, *"the second... solely because I mis-dispatched the first. So the round curve measures coordinator error here, not ticket difficulty."*

## 2. Well-supported, with a qualifier that is load-bearing

### 2.1 Fixing the measuring instrument before the step it scores — as its own isolated task

**The retrospective calls this "the practice I would keep if I could keep only one"** (`retrospective.md` §4.2). The isolation is the point: the instrument fix is a separate task with its own acceptance, so no refactoring agent can tune the metric it will be scored on.

**Evidence.** Four instances (R5a-0, R2-0, R1b, R1b2). **Three of four *deleted* more work than they created**, and one converted an unpassable step into a two-item to-do list: R5's cycle detector had no out-of-scope filter, so an explicitly-out-of-scope cycle counted and **no in-scope work could ever have turned R5 green**. A whole planned stage budgeted at ~34 tests "was never an R5 violation at all."

**The qualifier.** These figures are `[LOG]` in the retrospective's own labelling — taken from the contemporaneous decision log, not re-executed. No cost was recorded for any of the four.

**Why it holds anyway:** ten instrument defects are known on this one ticket, and **almost all were caught only by running something.** Defect 6 is the one that generalises: a field was named `matched_pattern` rather than `matched_rule` *so the detector would not count it* — **disclosed by the agent that did it, in its own report.** An honest agent still finds the cheap path. That is a property of goal-directed optimisation, not a discipline problem.

### 2.2 Recording a prediction before a step and scoring it honestly — but only against an instrument that can express both outcomes

**The practice works.** Five predictions recorded before D1a: two won, three lost, and **the losses were the informative ones** — prediction 1 lost *and the loss was the finding* (no assertion had ever pinned a test double's reason string). Canary predictions: three of four directionally right, and the one held most confidently was **right for the wrong reason** (`canary-results.md`). Across the surprise-factor series, on four of seven items **the estimator correctly predicted its own future misses by naming its uncertainties in advance** — "five for five, every miss described in advance, by mechanism."

**The qualifier that must travel with it, and it is the sharpest methodological lesson on the ticket.** R1 was pre-registered against the enrichment footprint's coupled-file count. That metric was **bounded below at ~7 by files that must legitimately name the field**, so R1 could not possibly have moved it to zero. **Scored on its stated criterion, the step that finally delivered R1's win would have been recorded as a failure.** It read correctly only because a scout checked what the metric *could* express before the step ran. (`retrospective.md` §5.2)

> **Pre-registering a criterion is necessary but not sufficient. The instrument must first be shown capable of expressing *both* outcomes.** A pre-registered criterion against an instrument that cannot move is not rigour — it is a false failure carrying the authority of advance commitment.

**Two further measured hazards.** (a) **Goodhart was observed directly inside a seven-item series**: on learning precision was being scored, the estimator narrowed from 25 predictions to 12, reasoning in writing that "hedging is what precision scoring punishes." (b) The protocol's own headline metric, `|A|/|P|`, **inverted against recall on every comparison where both were computed** and was dropped rather than kept "for continuity." (`TOO-45/reports/surprise-factor-protocol.md`)

**Where it did pay unambiguously:** predicting against a ticket revealed **two of seven items' own written justifications were measurably wrong** — one claimed sixteen call sites where there were eight; another claimed four sets where there were three live plus one dead, plus three undocumented copies the ticket never mentioned. Both were "written from a code reading rather than a count."

**The prediction apparatus's most-quoted output — and it does not survive verification.** The claim was that recall predicts cost: production recall ranged *"100% (tickets 74, 22, 85a) down to 15.2% (ticket 79)"*, ticket 79 being *"the campaign's most expensive item"* at *"11 agent runs, ~3M subagent tokens"*, therefore **"recall is a leading indicator of cost"** and the cheapest early warning available before work starts. **Corrected 2026-08-23 — every input to that inference is wrong or unsourced:**

- **The recall figures are the wrong column.** 15.2% and the three-item 100% list are the *all-files* numbers. On the production-only recomputation (`reports/surprise/PRODUCTION-ONLY-SCORING.md`), 79 scores **13.8%** and **five** items hit 100% (22, 74, 77, 78, 85). The mislabelling runs in the argument's own favour: under the production cut the measure saturates on a third of its cases, which is less discriminating, not more.
- **The cost ranking was retracted in the corpus itself** (`punch-list-2026-08-20.md:311`). Ticket 79 took **4h15m — below the phase-3 average and less than half of ticket 78**. The retraction was never carried forward. **78 is the real outlier at 8h51m.**
- **The agent-run figure is unsourced.** The only measurement is **7 runs / ~1.8M tokens** (`measurements/79-cost-assessment.md`, 08-21); 9/~2.6M and 11/~3M are later restatements that cite nothing. See `DURABLE/02-campaign-cost-data.md` C1.
- **"Expensive" was measured in the wrong currency.** Agent runs and tokens are a metric about the agent; wall-clock is the cost to Arnon. The two rank these tickets differently, and the argument silently used whichever number was larger.
- **The sources hedge where this sentence does not.** `open-questions.md:27`: the measure is *"weak and heavily confounded"* (ticket leak, design leak, scope impurity). `phase 3 resume.md:347`: low recall *"**may** be a leading indicator of cost — **test at the aggregate**"*. That aggregate test was never run.

**What remains after the correction: nothing usable.** The correlation was n=1, resting on a cost ranking that is refuted and a recall figure from the wrong column — and ticket 79 is also the *lowest-leak* item in the series, so its low recall is exactly what the **retracted** leak-level law would have predicted from leak alone. This is the corpus's clearest instance of a qualitative hedge being promoted to a measurement: *"may be a leading indicator — test at the aggregate"* became *"the cheapest early warning in this entire corpus."*

**The protocol's own failure modes, which are the reason to be careful with it.** Predictions leaked through the return channel **twice, through the same guard** — *"The guard added after item 05 was 'instruct the estimator to write to files and return only a token'. That instruction was simultaneously obeyed and defeated. **An instruction is not a mechanism.**"* A second leak route was the coordinator's own best habit: measuring a ticket before briefing it (*"this campaign's most productive practice — it closed 57 with zero work and corrected 20's diagnosis"*) wrote the measurements into the very files the blinded estimator reads; five tickets affected. Protocol steps were **silently skipped** on two tickets — *"skipping a protocol step silently is how a protocol stops meaning anything."* Three of four early scorings were taken from a working tree rather than the commit, and **it hid a real alarm**: item 04 reported "3 misses, all `E`, zero alarms" while omitting a test file that changed because `--eval` printed a deny verdict to stderr with empty stdout — **the existing test was pinning the bug.** And a stated law ("leak level predicts recall", monotone over 3 points) was **explicitly retracted** at 7 points: *"Three points looked like a law. Seven points look like a correlation with large residuals... The confidence came from the absence of counterexamples in a set I had not finished measuring."*

### 2.3 Executed scoping traces before implementing

**What it is.** A read-only investigation that *runs things* — renames a symbol and counts the wreckage, instruments a class's `__init__` and replays the corpus, builds a synthetic tree and feeds it to the detector — then restores byte-exactly and proposes a plan.

**Evidence.** Used on four steps. **It changed the plan every time, and twice it deleted more work than it created.** Concretely: R1's "free deletion" of two shims rested on a 0-caller count, and **actually deleting them broke 10 tests** ("free deletion is a 10-test deletion"); R2 gained a whole second instance the predicate structurally could not see, and a third nobody had found. (`retrospective.md` §4.1)

**The reusable toolkit is more valuable than any single finding**, and two items in it are underrated: **drift-guard live-fire counting** (3,996 index lookups under replay, the guard fired **0** times and the index answer never once disagreed — turning "is this defensive code earning its place?" from an argument into a measurement, and producing the ticket's cleanest deletion) and the **physical module-move probe** (rename the file, rewrite in-package paths only — `subagent` scored 684 on rename damage and **1** on actual move damage).

**The honest caveat, flagged by the retrospective itself as an open question:** *"none of the three traces records its own wall-clock or token cost, so the return on investment is qualitative."* **Next time, timestamp the trace.** **Noted 2026-08-23:** the source says *"Used on D1, R1, R5 and R2"* (**four**) in the same paragraph as *"none of the **three** traces"*. The inconsistency is the source's, not this summary's — but it means the "four steps" figure above should be read as the named list, not as something anybody counted.

**The discipline that made them safe:** byte-copy backup *before* any mutation, restore in a `finally`, verify by sha256, cross-check `git status --porcelain` against the session baseline. One trace records its own near-miss — a `sed` applied before the backup was taken, recovered only because the probe-name convention happened to be bijective.

**When it is NOT worth doing.** No negative instance recorded — which is itself a gap, since only four steps were traced and all four were large.

## 6. The finding most likely to be argued about

Two statements in this corpus point in opposite directions and both should survive — but they are not equally supported, and the correction below says which is which.

- The architecture-judge back-test found **eight live defects in code that had already passed review and shipped**, two serious enough to be marked fix-before-push.
- A claim in wide circulation in the campaign notes: **every architectural error caught on this ticket was caught by a human asking a direct question — none by any metric, blinded agent, or automated test.** **Corrected 2026-08-23 — this was previously presented as "the surprise-factor protocol's own closing admission", and it is not.** `TOO-45/reports/surprise-factor-protocol.md` does not close on it; it ends on the Q1/Q2 split and the per-item record, and its nearest line (`:193`) is the weaker *"Manual review is the control that works for architectural error ... Bugs, by contrast, *are* being caught by process improvements."* The chain runs auto-memory (`feedback_stop_at_first_working_boundary`) → `TOO-45 session resume.md:70` → `architecture-sweep-practices.md:67`, which is where the "closing admission" characterisation was *added*, → here. Ticket 85 carries an independent version, so the claim is not fabricated — but **§7 of this same document lists it among the figures sourceable only to auto-memory and says it should not be carried forward**, so it cannot also be offered here as one of two "well-supported statements". Treat it as a strongly-held campaign impression with one partial cross-check (below), not as a measured result.

The reconciliation the sources support: instruments found *local* defects reliably and **could not synthesise**. The synthesis gap was observed at four scales, independently — inside one function (two guards, each undetected alone because the single validation test trips both at once: *"neither mutation is a finding by itself; the pair is"*), across two tests pinning one behaviour (found by two agents hours apart, each filing "here is one weak test"), across three modules (severity ordering unpinned in three audit modules, found by three agents on three days, each filing a minor ticket), and **inside the coordinator's own process**, where the sweep's largest findings sat in a working-notes file for days.

That fourth instance is the one to take seriously: **this is not a limitation of subagents or of LLMs. It is a property of narrow, exhaustive attention as a method, and it applies to whatever is doing the narrow attending.**

Two mitigations, both cheap and both recorded as adopted:

1. **Schedule synthesis as its own step.** *"Synthesis over the whole finding set has to be a step in the skill, not a hope."*
2. **Promote a product-relevant finding into the decision-facing artifact the same day it is found, incomplete if necessary.** A defect recorded only in a working queue is a defect that will never be actioned.

And the human-side counterpart, which `corrections-analysis.md` calls **the cheapest fix in the whole document**: **voice unformed smells at quarter-confidence, and ask for them at boundaries.** Three of the five most-escalated themes were noticed early and raised late; on docstring bloat Arnon says outright *"it smelled. But I didn't raise it yet"*, and by the time he raised it the correction cost a sweep. The reciprocal is a question to ask at every boundary: **not "does this look right", but "is there anything bugging you that you haven't said."**
